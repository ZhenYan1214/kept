The national relief foundation publishes every transfer, which is more than most. Of 1.2 billion Taiwan dollars paid out, nearly ninety-nine percent was pre-allocated, most of it to one ministry. In eleven monthly reports, for eight months, not one more pre-allocated dollar was reported as reconciled. Pre-allocating is normal in an emergency. The problem is what comes next: after the first hand, the public sees only totals, and households have no public way to confirm or dispute their own payment." |
| N1 | 2 · Solution | **NEW** | The flow image (`kept-flow.png`) | "Kept is my answer to that gap. Every hand that holds the money signs for what it receives, and the last hand is the household. Each household can confirm or dispute its own payment on a public record. For those who never answer, a random sample is checked in person." |
| N2 | 3 · Technical architecture | **NEW** | The architecture image (`kept-architecture.png`), moving from the browser column to the server, the contracts and the off-chain strip as each is named | "Here is how it is built. The app runs in the browser. A household's identity and receipt key stay on its phone or a printed card, and its zero-knowledge proofs are made right there. A relayer pays the gas, so households never need crypto. On Base Sepolia, six contracts keep the record: a factory that creates each fund with a fixed charter, a custody ledger for every hand-over and payment line, a registry of households verified by two signatures, a voting module, and NGO programmes. Semaphore checks the proofs. Names, ID numbers and bank accounts never go on-chain." |
| 3 | 4 · Working prototype | reused row 3 | Demo: alerts → register a household with two signatures → publish payment lines → "I did not receive it" → dashboard and sample audits | "This is Kept, on a simulated version of the same disaster. Every hand acknowledges what it receives. When the ministry pays households, it publishes each payment as a line with no name on it. A household, verified by both the village chief and the township office, finds its own line without asking anyone, and says 'received', or 'not received'. The custodian cannot sign for the household, and cannot pad the list without it showing. And because many households never answer, the contract draws a random sample that an independent auditor visits in person." |
| 4 | 4 · Working prototype | reused row 4 | Demo: NGO counter, eligible once, rejected the second time | "An NGO can check eligibility on the spot with a zero-knowledge proof. The proof tells the NGO only that this is an eligible household that has not claimed yet; the NGO never receives the roster, and the same household cannot claim twice." |
| 5 | 4 · Working prototype | reused row 5 | Demo: outside the charter refused; cap change; household and neighbours vote no; "Vetoed by households" | "The fund's charter is fixed when the money is raised. Spending outside it is refused. Changes wait in public, and the people the money is for can veto them, anonymously, one vote per household." |
| N3 | 4 · Working prototype | **NEW** | Blockscout: the household's transaction with the decoded event `received = false`; the verified-contract badge | "All of this runs on the Base Sepolia testnet. It works under today's law, and at Guangfu's scale it costs about a hundred and fifty to fifteen hundred dollars on-chain." |
| N4 | 5 · Innovation & impact | **NEW** | Slide 08: what is new; the backtest; the path to adoption | "What is new is the household's side. Aid systems today record what organisations say they did. On real records, a backtest that assumes Hualien County had used Kept raises an alert 199 days before the first news story I found. And it can start without anyone's permission: volunteers can already turn a foundation's disclosures into open data. For the next step, I have drafted a county ordinance that would make it the rule." |
